# Story-led directing for A1–A6

Use this guide for A1–A6 when planning a new film, not as a template to impose on an existing approved cut. Each style still owns its drawing, narration, captions, sound and acceptance rules. A generated clip and an editorial shot are different: the default Flow source is 10 seconds, while the edit may use only the portion that serves the story. Preserve the requested film duration and natural speech; neither pad the film to a multiple of ten nor slow narration to fill a source clip. B Autopilot is outside this directing and Flow change.

## Find the story before choosing effects

Start with the audience and what they can see, want or fear. Identify the central question or tension, the change in understanding or situation, and the intended ending. Separate documented facts from speculation and fictional demonstrations; never improve a hook by inventing a statistic, a guaranteed outcome or a claim about every example. Honor a supplied script and ask before changing its substantive claims. A user's deliberate cliffhanger, visual payoff or quiet ending takes precedence over a generic call to comment.

Generate at least two materially different visual approaches internally when a scene feels generic. Choose the one with the clearest causal action, strongest relevance to the spoken idea and feasible production; do not add a mandatory alternatives presentation or new approval gate. Change the situation, viewpoint, reveal or choice, not just the color or camera zoom. Borrow genre pacing where useful without forcing a fixed five-stage arc onto a story that needs a different shape.

## Make each shot earn its place

For the opening, identify a legible first image and a visual or dramatic change in the first few seconds that makes the audience care about a specific person, object, contradiction or consequence. A narrated question alone over an idle scene is not a visual hook. Protect room for readable character acting and the narration; use an intentional hold when it carries emotion or information.

For each planned editorial shot, record as much as needed to make the idea shootable:

- **Narrative job and audience state:** what the audience understands, anticipates or feels before and after this shot; what question it raises, develops or answers.
- **Visible action and composition:** subject, setting, scale and viewpoint, start state, one primary action or reaction, result and what should be legible at mobile size. Change the image because the story advances, not on an arbitrary timer. Use a close-up, wide view, insert, reaction, point of view or deliberate hold when it adds meaning.
- **Sound and editorial connection:** the exact narration interval, relevant sound, in/out states and cut or continuity relationship to the neighboring shot. Let images contribute evidence, tension, irony or consequence rather than merely repeat the narration. Do not force every beat into the same three-part formula, fixed number of props, visual devices or camera moves.
- **Execution and risk:** the reference, source clip and proposed usable interval; a simple action, clear contact, preserved prop and screen direction. Record uncertain or failure-prone details and a story-appropriate lower-risk shot or cutaway. Separate grabbing, carrying and releasing where doing so improves reliability and readability.

Work from the actual narration and naturally varying shot lengths. A ten-second source can be cut into shorter, different editorial beats when its footage supports them; multiple source clips may be needed for a dense or physically difficult beat. Plan enough footage and handles without treating every transition as an excuse to generate a new clip. Respect style-specific faster-cut or hold rules.

## Check the director's cut

Before generating, mentally watch the storyboard without its narration: can the viewer follow the relationship between subject, action and consequence? Then read the narration without the images: are the claims, surprise and payoff supported? Check that each shot changes knowledge, stakes, emotion or spatial understanding and that the visual sequence has purposeful variety, rather than a repeated person-next-to-object composition. Remove filler and reconcile confusing props, entrances and transitions.

After generation, inspect the actual motion and cuts, not only prompts or sparse stills. Check hands, object identity, plausible contact, entrances/exits, unintended background movement, mouth behavior and cut boundaries. Use clean trims and unaffected footage before regenerating; retry only failed scope within the authorized budget. A background-only stabilization may be used when it preserves staging and is disclosed, but do not mask, repaint, replace or hide a speaking mouth to manufacture a pass. Report unresolved faults and distinguish technical checks from continuous visual and listening review. This process improves craft; it does not guarantee model fidelity, audience retention or a specific completion rate.
